Endives au gratin

Witloof met hesp en kaassausWIT-lohf met HESP en KAHS-sowsChicons au gratinshee-koh(n) oh grah-TA(n)

Belgian endive is grown in the dark, which keeps its leaves pale and tender. Its names are a lesson in Belgian vocabulary: witloof in Flanders (witlof in the Netherlands), chicon in French-speaking Belgium, endive in France. For this dish the heads are cooked, wrapped in a slice of ham (hesp in Flemish), covered in béchamel or cheese sauce, sprinkled with grated cheese and baked until golden.

It is a hot main course, a classic of Belgian home cooking and especially of winter, often served with fries and a salad. The cooked endive keeps a gentle bitterness that goes well with the ham and cheese.

Witloof was first grown around Brussels in the 19th century, and Schaerbeek is usually named as its birthplace; how exactly it began is not known, and the stories about farmers hiding chicory roots in their cellars are considered folk tales. Brussels grondwitloof, grown in soil the traditional way, has been an EU protected geographical indication since 2008, and endives are on Brussels’ list of intangible heritage.

How to eat it

It comes out of the oven bubbling hot, so give it a minute. Cut each roll in pieces and scoop up some sauce with every bite.
