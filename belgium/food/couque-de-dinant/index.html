Couque de Dinant

Couque de Dinantkook duh dee-NAH(n)Couque de Dinantkook duh dee-NAH(n)

The couque de Dinant is traditionally made of just two ingredients, wheat flour and honey, in equal weights, with no water or yeast. The dough is pressed into carved wooden moulds showing animals, flowers, people or landscapes and baked at around 300 °C, so the honey caramelises. Once it has cooled, the couque is extremely hard, and it keeps almost indefinitely.

It is the speciality of Dinant, on the Meuse, where a few family bakeries still make it; one has been at it since 1860. A popular story links its origin to the siege of Dinant in 1466, but historians doubt it, and the couque only appears in sources in the 18th century.

A softer, sweeter cousin with added sugar is called couque de Rins.

How to eat it

Don’t bite into it: you could break a tooth. Break off a small piece and let it melt slowly in your mouth, or dip it in coffee to soften it.
